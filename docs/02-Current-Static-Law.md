# **2️⃣ Current Static Law**

## **🧱 Introduction**
This document provides a **neutral, factual explanation** of how static law is currently created and amended in the United Kingdom.  
It does **not** criticise Parliament, the House of Lords, or the legislative process.  
Instead, it describes the **structural behaviour** of static law so readers can understand why it often becomes outdated by the time it is enacted.

This forms the foundation for understanding why a polymorphic legal model behaves differently.

---

# **1️⃣ The Static Law Lifecycle (UK Context)**

Static law follows a **linear, fixed sequence**.  
Each stage assumes the **problem**, the **environment**, and the **conditions** will remain stable throughout the drafting and approval process.

---

## **🔍 Step 1 — A Problem Is Identified**
A societal, technological, economic, or organisational issue emerges that requires formal resolution.

At this stage, the structural assumption is:

> **The problem will remain the same throughout the legislative process.**

This assumption is important because it shapes every subsequent step.

---

## **📝 Step 2 — A Static Law Is Drafted**
Policy teams and legal drafters create a new law based on the **current** understanding of the problem.

The draft embeds:
- fixed definitions  
- fixed thresholds  
- fixed conditions  
- fixed interpretations  

Static law is written as a **snapshot** of the environment at the moment of drafting.

> **It is written once, based on the present — but applied to the future.**

---

## **🔁 Step 3 — Commons ↔ Lords Iteration Loop**
The draft enters the legislative cycle.

It must pass through:
- multiple readings  
- committee stages  
- amendments  
- scrutiny  
- debate  
- agreement between both Houses  

The Commons and Lords may send the bill back and forth **many times** until both agree on the final wording.

This loop can take:
- months  
- years  
- occasionally longer  

During this period, the **environment continues to evolve**, but the draft law does not.

---

## **📜 Step 4 — Ratification**
Once both Houses agree, the bill receives Royal Assent and becomes law.

At this moment, the law is:
- legally valid  
- democratically authorised  
- procedurally complete  

But it is also **historic**.

The environment has already changed since the law was drafted, debated, amended, and approved.

> **By the time a static law is ratified, the environment it was designed for may no longer exist.**

---

# **2️⃣ Why Static Law Becomes Historic**

Static law is not flawed — it is structurally slow because it must be:
- debated  
- scrutinised  
- amended  
- agreed  
- ratified  

This ensures democratic legitimacy and legal stability.

But it also means:
- definitions may become outdated  
- thresholds may no longer match reality  
- conditions may no longer apply  
- loopholes may emerge  
- enforcement may become inconsistent  

This is not a criticism — it is a **structural property** of static law.

---

```
┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│                    STATIC LAW LIFECYCLE                      │
└──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

   🔍 Problem Identified
           │
           ▼
   📝 Draft Law Created
   (based on current environment)
           │
           ▼
   🔁 Commons ↔ Lords Iteration Loop
   (months / years of debate and amendment)
           │
           ▼
   📜 Law Ratified
   (environment has already evolved)
           │
           ▼
   ⏳ Law Becomes Historic
   - definitions fixed
   - thresholds fixed
   - conditions fixed
   - environment changed
```

### **Diagram Summary**
Static law is drafted for the **past**, implemented in the **present**, and applied to the **future**.

This mismatch is the core structural limitation.

This document provides a neutral, factual explanation of how static law is created in the UK.

Its purpose is to show:

> **Static law is structurally fixed and structurally slow.  
> The environment is structurally dynamic and structurally fast.**

This minnnnnnsmatch is what motivates the exploration of polymorphic law.
